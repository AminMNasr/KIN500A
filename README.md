2 Introduction

Neural delays are inherent in the control of standing balance; they represent the time lag between sensing environmental changes and generating motor responses to maintain stability [1]. These delays, typically up to 160 milliseconds, occur due to the time required for signals to travel between from sensory end organs to the CNS and from the CNS to the muscles to generate forces. The CNS must account for these delays to ensure timely and accurate balance adjustments [56]. Rasman et al. [21] used a robotic balance simulator to test whether healthy participants could adapt to artificially imposed sensorimotor delays when balancing upright. The study found that despite initial destabilization and reduced sensory contributions, participants re-learned to balance with delays up to 560 milliseconds through five days of training and retained this ability after three months. These findings suggest that the CNS can adapt to extended neural delays.

In an attempt to model the control of humans with neural delays larger than 500 ms, Rasman and colleagues [58] developed an advanced version of deterministic controllers, incorporating a Smith predictor based on the work by Miall and colleagues [57]. They utilized an LQR to model the control of standing balance with a predictor designed to account for delays. The model integrated physiological sensory (ΔtS = 60 ms) and motor (ΔtM = 60 ms) delays, along with an artificial robotic delay (ΔtR = up to 350 ms) to align with their experimental procedures. To solve the control problem, they derived the controller's gain by minimizing a cost function that weighs the system states (angular position and velocity) and control efforts (torque). The pendulum control problem yielded controller gains while accounting for uncertainties from noises. Also, delays were represented in the continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation via Padé approximations. This controller operated on the sum of feedback error with a gain k and an error based on a predicted state by the Smith predictor and setpoint. The Smith predictor was used to anticipate the consequences of motor actions and effectively regulate the system; however, this requires having a forward internal model which is a useful engineering tool, but it may not have a direct counterpart in the CNS.

Efferent copies of commands, also known as corollary discharge signals, play a crucial role in motor neural control [11]. These copies allow the CNS to anticipate the effects of motor commands before sensory feedback arrives and might be effective in compensating for delays in the sensory and motor pathways. By accessing this internal information, the brain can adjust its control strategies in real-time, enabling more accurate and timely responses to maintain balance and stability. Copies of commands are not commonly used in the control models, however, in conventional models, a copy of torque output is used as feedback to the control model. The purpose of this torque feedback was to replace the setpoint to compensate for the low-frequency oscillations [41]. In sum, inserting efferent copies of commands to the controller of standing balance might help the controller handle delays since it can predict the consequence of actions, or in other words, future states.


I will use RL features to establish a framework to handle delays and incorporate the copies of efferent commands into the control rule. I hypothesize that a continuous form (not Q-learning) of the RL framework can handle neural delays and explain the control rule in unexpected sensorimotor delay conditions by using efferent copies of commands. Chen and colleagues [89] introduced a delay-aware reinforcement learning framework that transforms delayed systems into standard MDPs using augmented states, demonstrating more efficient training and transferability in delayed environments compared to model-free methods. Their experiments using the Gym and MuJoCo platforms demonstrate that the augmented state system improves training efficiency and enhances transferability across systems with different delay durations. Gym provides a suite of benchmark environments for developing and comparing reinforcement learning algorithms, while MuJoCo (Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact) is a physics engine used for simulating detailed, continuous control tasks. In sum, they increased the number of states by incorporating a representation of actions in the inputs of the RL model. I will implement this concept in the context of standing balance to enhance my RL framework and handle neural delays. 


3 Proposed method

3-1 Change to policy gradient 
	
I will change the current RL model to a continuous version. In a continuous form of RL (such as online or incremental policy gradient [8]), the function will be updated for all possible states in the system. Vanilla policy gradient (VPG) is a reinforcement learning technique commonly used to directly maximize policies through maximizing the expected cumulative rewards. Unlike value-based approaches, which involve estimating the value function, the VPG method directly optimizes the policy by adjusting its parameters to increase the likelihood of selecting actions that yield higher rewards. It implements a Monte Carlo sampling technique to capture complete paths (state-action-reward sequences) and calculates the gradient of policy parameters according to the rewards that it detects in the paths.
	
A vanilla policy gradient method that changes at each step rather than every episode is often referred to as an online or incremental policy gradient method. In contrast to the vanilla policy gradient, which waits for an episode final return to calculate the total return and update the policy, the incremental policy gradient approach updates the policy every observation so that it immediately responds to any new data. This incremental updating can help increase the learning pace and stability, particularly during longer episodes where delayed updates would stall the information about the reward. I will use the incremental policy gradient approach to develop the standing balance controller. In this approach, instead of using the return (the sum of future rewards over the entire trajectory from time), it will use the advantage or temporal difference (TD) error to approximate the immediate reward-to-go at each step.
